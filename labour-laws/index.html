Statutory Labour Legislation Across the Six GCC Member States

A comprehensive comparative legal review of private-sector employment statutes, end of service benefits, notice period obligations, and worker safeguards across the United Arab Emirates, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, State of Kuwait, State of Qatar, Sultanate of Oman, and Kingdom of Bahrain.

1. The Evolution of Private Sector Employment Law in the Gulf

The employment landscape across the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) has undergone profound statutory modernisation over the past decade. National development roadmaps, such as Saudi Vision 2030, UAE Centennial 2071, and Oman Vision 2040, have driven sweeping legislative revisions. The historical kafala (sponsorship) framework has been substantially dismantled or reformed, replaced by standardized civil employment contracts, enhanced mobility protocols, and strict wage protection systems (WPS).

Despite overarching economic coordination among GCC member nations, private-sector employment rights and terminal financial accruals remain strictly governed by distinct national statutes. Significant disparities exist in how daily wages are computed, how resignation penalties are structured, and whether terminal benefits are capped. Understanding these statutory distinctions is essential for corporate legal counsel, human resource directors, and the millions of expatriate professionals operating in the region.

3. Detailed Country-by-Country Legal Analysis

United Arab Emirates: Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021

Promulgated in February 2022, Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021 repealed the four-decade-old Federal Law No. 8 of 1980. The modern legislation applies to all private-sector establishments across the mainland and non-financial free zones. Financial free zones, specifically the Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC) and Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM), maintain separate common-law employment frameworks.

Under Article 51 of Decree-Law No. 33/2021, expatriate employees completing at least one continuous year of service accrue terminal benefits at twenty-one calendar days of basic wage for each of the first five years, and thirty calendar days for each subsequent year. Crucially, the reform abolished the historical reduction tiers that previously penalized resigning workers. Today, an employee resigning voluntarily receives identical statutory severance to an employee terminated without cause. The maximum aggregate award is capped at two years' basic salary.

Kingdom of Saudi Arabia: Royal Decree No. M/51

The Saudi Labour Law, promulgated under Royal Decree No. M/51 and administered by the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development (MHRSD), governs private-sector employment across the Kingdom. Articles 84 through 88 dictate the calculation of the End of Service Award (Mukafoat Nihayat Al-Khidma).

A fundamental distinction of Saudi law is the wage basis. Article 2 mandates that the award must be computed on the worker's Actual Wage (Al-Ajr Al-Fi'li), which comprises basic salary plus all fixed recurring allowances, including housing and transportation. Under Article 84, the award accrues at half of one month's wage per year for the first five years, and one full month's wage per year thereafter.

Unlike the UAE, Saudi Arabia retains statutory resignation deduction tiers under Article 85. If an employee resigns with an indefinite contract, they forfeit all entitlements if tenure is under two years, receive one-third between two and five years, receive two-thirds between five and ten years, and receive full entitlement after ten completed years. Article 87 provides explicit statutory exemptions where full severance is paid upon resignation, such as female workers resigning within six months of marriage or three months of childbirth.

State of Kuwait: Private Sector Labour Law No. 6 of 2010

In Kuwait, employment relations in the private sector are regulated by Law No. 6 of 2010. The statute contains one of the most worker-favorable computational requirements in the entire Middle East.

Under Article 55, the statutory daily wage for monthly paid workers must be calculated by dividing monthly remuneration by twenty-six working days, accounting for weekly rest days. Generic calculators dividing by thirty calendar days commit an unlawful reduction of approximately 13.3 percent. Article 51 establishes accrual at fifteen working days per year for the first five years, and thirty working days per year thereafter. The total award cannot exceed one and a half years' remuneration (eighteen months of salary). Voluntary resignation under Article 53 triggers deduction tiers: zero entitlement under three years, fifty percent between three and five years, two-thirds between five and ten years, and full entitlement at ten years.

State of Qatar: Labour Law No. 14 of 2004

The State of Qatar codified employment rights under Law No. 14 of 2004, supplemented by major ministerial decrees dismantling exit permits and establishing a non-discriminatory statutory minimum wage.

Article 54 provides that end-of-service gratuity is mandatory for all workers completing at least one continuous year of service. The statutory minimum accrual rate is three weeks (twenty-one calendar days) of basic wage for each completed year, plus pro-rata fractions for partial months. Unlike Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, Qatar does not impose statutory percentage deductions for voluntary resignation once the initial one-year service threshold is met.

Sultanate of Oman: Royal Decree No. 53/2023

On July 25, 2023, His Majesty Sultan Haitham bin Tarik promulgated the new Oman Labour Law via Royal Decree No. 53/2023, formally repealing Royal Decree No. 35/2003. This landmark reform restructured expatriate severance benefits.

Under Article 61 of the new law, non-Omani workers receive a flat one full month's basic salary (thirty days' wage) for every completed year of service, starting from their first year. The old bifurcation (fifteen days for the first three years) was permanently eliminated for post-decree service. Additionally, resignation does not trigger statutory penalties under compliant notice execution.

Kingdom of Bahrain: Law No. 36 of 2012

In Bahrain, private-sector employment is governed by Law No. 36 of 2012. Article 116 dictates that expatriate employees not covered by national social insurance pension schemes receive end of service indemnity equal to fifteen days' wage per year for the first three years of service, and one full month's wage (thirty days) for each subsequent year.

A transformative regulatory advancement took effect under the Social Insurance Organisation (SIO) reform, requiring employers to remit monthly expatriate indemnity deposits directly into a central SIO account. This institutional mechanism guarantees that severance balances remain fully funded and protected against enterprise insolvency.

4. Statutory Notice Periods and Termination Safeguards

Across the GCC, employment termination requires strict compliance with statutory notice periods. The standard statutory baseline is thirty calendar days for monthly paid workers, though contracts may mutually specify longer periods (frequently sixty or ninety days for executive personnel).

Summary dismissal without notice or gratuity is strictly confined to exhaustive statutory codes: Article 120 of the historical UAE law (now codified under Article 44 of Decree-Law 33/2021), Article 80 in Saudi Arabia, Article 41 in Kuwait, Article 61 in Qatar, and Article 40 in Oman. These grounds require substantiated material breaches, such as falsification of identity, severe commercial misconduct resulting in substantial financial loss, or physical violence during work. In the absence of an Article 80/44 justification, termination without statutory notice obligates the employer to remit compensation in lieu of notice equal to the worker's full contractual wage for the notice duration.

5. Expatriate Workplace Savings Alternatives (DEWS & SIO Funds)

The traditional GCC gratuity model relies on unfunded corporate liabilities, where employers disburse lump sums directly from cash flow upon contract termination. This model is rapidly evolving toward structured workplace savings schemes.

In the UAE, the Dubai International Financial Centre launched the DIFC Employee Workplace Savings (DEWS) scheme, requiring employers to deposit monthly contributions into independent trust accounts managed by professional asset managers. Following DEWS, the UAE Federal Government launched an optional alternative gratuity savings scheme under Cabinet Resolution No. 96 of 2023. Similarly, Bahrain's SIO central deposit protocol guarantees full employee liquidity upon separation. These institutional systems represent the future of pension-style terminal benefits across the Gulf.
